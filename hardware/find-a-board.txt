Where I am: [YOUR COUNTRY].   Budget: [YOUR BUDGET].

HARD REQUIREMENTS. A board that misses any of these is no use to me.

  - A 64-bit RISC-V application processor, RV64GC (rv64imafdc), running Linux. Not a
    microcontroller: it has to boot Debian, Ubuntu or similar from microSD or eMMC and
    let me log in over SSH via Ethernet or WiFi.

  - Working hardware performance counters under Linux `perf`. This is the one I cannot
    work around, and it is the one most likely to be wrong. The test is:

        perf stat -e cycles,instructions -- true

    It must print real counts, not `<not supported>` and not zeroes. On RISC-V the
    counters reach Linux through the firmware, so this depends on OpenSBI exposing the
    SBI PMU extension and the kernel having CONFIG_RISCV_PMU_SBI — it is a property of
    the software image as much as of the chip. For each board, tell me what evidence
    you actually have: a forum post, bug report or article showing real `perf stat`
    output is worth far more than a spec sheet, and a spec sheet saying the core has a
    hardware performance monitor does not mean a shipping image exposes it.

  - At least 4 GB of RAM, 8 GB preferred.

  - At least 2 cores, 4 or more preferred — one chapter measures what cores cost each
    other, and that needs more than two.

STRONGLY PREFERRED

  - An in-order core, such as the SiFive U74 family. The book explains microarchitecture
    by measuring it, and an in-order core makes the effects legible. An aggressive
    out-of-order core is not disqualifying, but it muddies small experiments.

  - Currently in production and still receiving distro images, rather than end-of-life
    or a board whose vendor kernel was abandoned two years ago.

NICE TO HAVE, roughly in order

  - An M.2 slot for NVMe. Builds and the file-system chapters are much less tedious.
  - RVV 1.0 vector support. One chapter currently reasons about vectorisation because
    the reference hardware cannot do it; a vector-capable board would let me measure it.
  - A 3.3 V UART header, for watching a boot that never reaches the network.
